        The Nitchū Line, a cherry path

        About the Nitchū-sen Weeping Cherry Trees (Kitakata)A single spring journey,
        following the abandoned line.

        The Nitchū-sen Weeping Cherry Trees (Nicchusen Rows of Weeping Cherry Trees) are a cherry promenade in the center of Kitakata, Fukushima. Built on the right-of-way of the former JNR Nitchū Line, about 3 km long, it is both a local living street and one of Japan's finest weeping-cherry spots, visited by many travelers.

        About a 5-minute walk west from Kitakata Station. The section of the former JNR Nitchū Line that once linked Kitakata and Atsushio is now a local pedestrian and cycling path, and a promenade where people wait for spring.

        Pass under the weeping branches, meet the black SL, and go further north. About 3 km that shifts from bustle to quiet—walk it at your own pace.

        From railway to flower path

        History & Significance of the Nitchū-sen Weeping Cherry TreesHow it became
        a spring path.

        1938

        Nitchū Line opens

        A local railway of about 11.6 km linking Kitakata and Atsushio, supporting people's lives and travel.

        1984

        Entire line abolished

        It finished its role at the end of March; part of the right-of-way was transformed into a pedestrian and cycling path for citizens.

        1987

        Pedestrian path planned

        Designated in city planning as a 3,190 m road, with rest spaces and the SL Plaza developed over time.

        Today

        About 1,000 springs

        Where railway memory and citizens' daily life overlap, it has become a representative spring landscape of Kitakata.

        Learn more deeply

        How this road became
        a cherry path.

        We introduce separately the historical facts confirmed in public materials and what is passed down as the region's memory. Years and expressions may differ by source.

        01

        The whereabouts of the name "Nitchū Line"

        The local railway of about 11.6 km linking Kitakata and Atsushio opened in 1938 and was fully abolished at the end of March 1984. There are theories about the origin of the line name based on regional names, but primary sources are limited. What is certain is that the name lives on today in facilities like the "Nitchū-sen Memorial Cycling and Pedestrian Path" and the "Nitchū-sen Memorial Hall."

        02

        The black locomotive C11 63

        The C11 at SL Plaza is a small steam locomotive the JNR used on branch lines. It supported both passengers and freight and suited hilly local lines. Now preserved among the cherries, each spring the contrast of blossoms and black body becomes a symbol of this place.

        03

        When the track became a living street

        After abolition, part of the track land was developed as a citizen cycling and pedestrian path, and on February 24, 1987 it was designated in city planning at 3,190 m long and 7–10 m wide. As a reuse case that converted railway infrastructure directly into a "people's path," it still draws attention from across the country.

        04

        About 1,000 weeping cherries

        Along with the path, weeping varieties were planted—about 1,000 in all. The drooping branches spread over pedestrians' heads and turn the road into a "cherry tunnel." This brief spring scene, tied to Kitakata's annual events, leads to today's Cherry Blossom Festival.

        05

        Storehouses, clear water, and a noodle town

        Kitakata's town center still has varied warehouse architecture—white plaster, black plaster, brick—used as shops, homes, and sake breweries. Combined with groundwater from the Iide mountains and the "morning ramen" culture, cherry season becomes one continuous trip of blossoms and food.

        06

        Not a "legend," but a stack of facts

        No old legend tied to a specific tree is confirmed for this row. What exists is the memory of a railway that supported the region, the decision of how to use an abandoned line, and the time citizens have spent nurturing the cherries. Precisely because there is no flashy story, this path continues as an extension of daily life.

        How to get here by mode

        How to Get to the Nitchū-sen Weeping Cherry Trees in KitakataAir · rail · car · taxi.

        Use "JR Kitakata Station" or "South Entrance" as your destination. Travel times vary with connections and road conditions, so check the official transfer guides and timetables before departure.

        01 By airplane

        Transfer from the airport by public transit

        • Nearest airports: Fukushima Airport and Sendai Airport (both need rail/bus connections).
        • From Fukushima Airport, connection bus + train to Kitakata Station; about 2.5–3.5 hours.
        • From Sendai Airport, airport rail + Shinkansen + local line; about 3–4 hours.
        • Late at night, connections are limited. Check the last service first.

        02 By rail

        Get off at JR Ban'etsu-sai Line, Kitakata Station

        • About a 5-minute walk west from Kitakata Station to the south entrance; the station is convenient as the southern start.
        • About 20–25 min by local train from Aizu-Wakamatsu Station; connect via the Ban'etsu-sai Line from Koriyama or Niigata.
        • Some daytime hours have few trains. Check your return time first.
        • Use the station's ticket gates, coin lockers, etc., per station guidance.

        03 By bus

        Route buses & temporary shuttles

        • Route buses run from Aizu-Wakamatsu toward Kitakata town (limited frequency).
        • Some festival years run temporary shuttles; check the year's official info.
        • In bloom season, road congestion causes delays—allow extra time.

        04 By car

        From the Ban'etsu Expressway

        • About 25–30 min from Aizu-Wakamatsu IC to Kitakata town.
        • Set "Kitakata Station" or the year's announced temporary parking as your navigation destination.
        • Don't set the middle of the row as destination; nearby is a living street with no roadside parking.
        • Congestion in bloom season; arriving early is smoothest.

        05 By taxi

        Short hop from the station front

        • A taxi stand is at Kitakata Station front (standard station-front layout).
        • Only a few minutes from the station to the south entrance; handy with luggage or in rain.
        • Demand peaks in bloom season; expect a wait or book ahead.
        • At hard-to-catch return times, ask the station for a dispatch contact.

        06 Rental car · bicycle

        For touring around

        • Rental cars are typically offices near Aizu-Wakamatsu Station or in Kitakata; book early in bloom season.
        • The path allows bicycles, but pedestrians first. Push your bike when crowded.
        • Rental-cycle availability varies by year; check city/tourism-association info.
        • Suited to a day trip combining the storehouse town and breweries.

        Travel times vary with connections, road conditions, and season. This does not recommend any specific operator or service. Check each carrier's official timetable and transfer guide before departure.

        Know before you walk

        How cherries work and
        the visitor's responsibility.

        As a non-profit science guide, we summarize how blossoms work and the actions to leave this place for next spring.

        Botany

        The weeping cherry form

        "Weeping cherry" is a general name for cherries whose branches bow downward in an arch—a line maintained horticulturally. Instead of growing up, they spread branches downward, so rows from both sides of a path easily overlap and wrap the walking space in blossoms. The reason this row is called a "tunnel" lies in the very choice of variety.

        Botany

        What decides when they bloom

        Cherries wake from winter dormancy with low temperatures (dormancy breaking), then buds grow and bloom from accumulated warmth (degree days). So "when they bloom" is naturally decided by the history of winter cold and spring warmth, shifting each year. Recently, earlier blooming is reported especially in cities; yearly records at the same spot are valuable clues.

        Environment

        Abandoned line as a "green corridor"

        A long, continuous green strip works as a corridor helping wildlife move through the city. This place, converting continuous former-rail land directly into a greenway, serves recreation while also linking nearby nature. It also softens summer glare and offers pedestrians shade.

        Wildlife

        First nectar source of spring

        Cherry blossoms in early spring are among the season's first resources for insects seeking nectar and pollen. From insects active at bloom, to birds after them, to fallen petals and leaves returning to soil, the row holds a small ecosystem. Not breaking branches or bark protects this cycle.

        Records

        Your observation becomes a source

        Recording bloom day, full-bloom day, and first-fall day with that day's weather becomes primary information valuable to next year's visitors. "Fixed-point observation" of the same spot and same individual each year also means citizen-participation records capturing climate change.

        Responsibility

        What visitors keep

        • Don't break branches, climb trunks, or pick blossoms.
        • This is a living street. Don't enter private land, residences, or shop parking.
        • Take trash home. Mind smoking, noise, and early/late conversations.
        • Don't block the path with tripods or gear. Yield the spot when a line forms.
        • No drones over private land, as a safety and privacy principle.
        • Always pick up after pets; keep the leash short.
        • Don't bring in plants or creatures (to avoid disturbing native vegetation).

        Frequently asked questions

        before you go.

        Where are the Nitchū-sen Weeping Cherry Trees?

        They are in Kitakata, Fukushima (966-0896, Oshikirihigashi 2-chome and others). About a 5-minute walk west from JR Kitakata Station, and about 25–30 minutes by car from the Aizu-Wakamatsu Interchange. Coordinates: 37.6549877 N, 139.8661643 E.

        How long does it take to walk the whole path?

        One-way walking alone is about 40–60 minutes. Allow around 2 hours including photos, breaks, the walk to and from the station or parking, and turning back.

        Can I walk with a stroller or elderly visitors?

        Basically a flat pedestrian and cycling path. When crowded, consider a shorter route turning back at SL Plaza.

        When is the best time to see the blossoms?

        Usually mid to late April, but it varies with temperature. Check the current-year bloom status announced by Kitakata City just before your visit.

        Is there nighttime illumination?

        Sections and hours change yearly. In years or periods without an official announcement, don't assume illumination will be in place.

        Is there an admission fee? Can I walk for free?

        Strolling is free. The Nitchū-sen Weeping Cherry Trees are a public promenade, free year-round. During the Cherry Blossom Festival, a cooperation fee may be requested for temporary parking and restroom upkeep.

        Is there parking?

        No permanent large tourist parking nearby. Temporary parking may be set during the festival; location, period, eligible vehicles, and fee change every year. Check Kitakata City's official guidance for the year.

        What is the Google Maps rating?

        As of the September 2026 sync, the Google Maps user rating is 4.3 out of 5, with 2,218 reviews. Ratings fluctuate, so check the latest on Google Maps' official page↗.

        Are there restrooms along the path?

        Public restrooms are along the path, and general ones at JR Kitakata Station and public/commercial facilities in town. Temporary toilets are added in some festival years. As numbers are limited, use them early near the station or entrance.

        Can I enjoy it on a rainy day?

        On rainy days it is less crowded and the weeping cherries look deeper in color when wet. Bring an umbrella or raincoat and non-slip shoes; protect your feet and gear. In strong wind or thunderstorms, don't push it—consider covered town facilities.

        Is there fuel or EV charging nearby?

        Gas stations are along town streets and arterials. EV quick chargers tend to be at roadside stations, commercial facilities, and dealerships, but availability and connectors vary. Confirm via official charging-network info before visiting.
